# Overview

Asthma is a chronic disease of the lungs that affects the airways, which are the tubes that carry air into and out of the lungs.

In asthma, the airways can become inflamed and narrowed. This can make it harder for air to move through the lungs and can cause symptoms such as coughing, wheezing, shortness of breath, and chest tightness.

When asthma symptoms become worse than usual, this is called an asthma attack or flare-up.

Asthma can affect people of all ages and often begins during childhood, although it can also begin during adulthood.

# Causes

The exact cause of asthma is not known.

Genetic and environmental factors both appear to play a role in whether a person develops asthma.

Asthma symptoms can become worse when a person is exposed to substances or situations called asthma triggers.

Different people can have different triggers, and a person's triggers can change over time.

# Types and Triggers

## Allergic Asthma

Allergic asthma occurs when asthma symptoms are triggered by allergens.

Common allergens include:

- Dust mites
- Mold
- Pets
- Pollen from grass, trees, and weeds
- Cockroach and mouse waste

## Nonallergic Asthma

Nonallergic asthma can be triggered by things that are not allergens.

Possible triggers include:

- Cold air
- Certain medicines
- Household chemicals
- Respiratory infections such as colds and flu
- Outdoor air pollution
- Tobacco smoke

## Occupational Asthma

Occupational asthma can occur when a person is exposed to chemicals, industrial dust, or other substances at work that trigger asthma symptoms.

## Exercise-Related Symptoms

Physical exercise can trigger asthma symptoms in some people, particularly when the air is dry.

# Common Asthma Triggers

Asthma triggers vary from person to person.

Common triggers can include:

- Allergens
- Dust
- Mold
- Pollen
- Pets
- Cockroaches
- Respiratory infections
- Cold or dry air
- Tobacco smoke
- Air pollution
- Certain chemicals
- Certain medicines
- Physical exercise

Identifying and avoiding personal triggers can be an important part of asthma management.

# Risk Factors

Factors that can increase the risk of asthma include:

- Family history of asthma
- Genetic factors
- Allergies
- Obesity
- Exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke
- Exposure to certain chemicals or industrial dusts at work
- Repeated viral respiratory infections during childhood
- Certain environmental exposures

Asthma can occur in people without an obvious family history or known risk factor.

# Symptoms

Common symptoms of asthma include:

- Wheezing
- Coughing
- Shortness of breath
- Chest tightness

Coughing may be especially noticeable at night or early in the morning.

Symptoms can range from mild to severe.

Some people experience symptoms every day, while others have symptoms only occasionally.

# Asthma Attacks

An asthma attack occurs when asthma symptoms become significantly worse.

During an attack, the airways can become more narrowed and inflamed, making breathing more difficult.

Asthma attacks can develop gradually or suddenly.

Symptoms during an attack can include:

- Severe coughing
- Increased wheezing
- Significant shortness of breath
- Difficulty breathing
- Increased chest tightness

Severe asthma attacks can be life-threatening.

People who experience repeated asthma attacks may need their treatment plan reviewed by a health care provider.

# Diagnosis

A health care provider may use several methods to diagnose asthma.

These can include:

- Medical history
- Physical examination
- Lung function tests
- Spirometry
- Peak expiratory flow testing
- Tests that measure how the airways respond to specific exposures
- Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) testing
- Allergy skin tests
- Allergy blood tests

## Spirometry

Spirometry is a lung function test that measures how well a person can move air in and out of the lungs.

It can help a health care provider evaluate airflow limitation and assess whether asthma may be present.

# Treatment and Management

Asthma treatment aims to control symptoms, prevent asthma attacks, and maintain normal lung function.

Treatment plans vary depending on the person's condition and severity.

A health care provider may recommend:

- Avoiding known asthma triggers
- Quick-relief medicines
- Long-term controller medicines
- Regular monitoring
- An individualized asthma action plan

# Quick-Relief Medicines

Quick-relief medicines are used to relieve asthma symptoms quickly, including symptoms that occur during an asthma attack.

Many quick-relief medicines work by relaxing the muscles around the airways and helping open them.

A health care provider determines which medicine is appropriate and how it should be used.

# Controller Medicines

Controller medicines are taken regularly to help prevent asthma symptoms and asthma attacks.

Some controller medicines work by reducing inflammation in the airways.

They are generally used regularly according to the treatment plan, even when symptoms are not present.

A health care provider determines which controller medicine is appropriate.

# Avoiding Triggers

Avoiding known triggers can help reduce asthma symptoms.

Examples include:

- Avoiding tobacco smoke
- Reducing exposure to known allergens
- Managing exposure to workplace chemicals or dust
- Taking precautions during periods of high air pollution
- Managing respiratory infections
- Following the asthma management plan provided by a health care professional

Trigger avoidance should be individualized because triggers differ between people.

# Asthma and Exercise

Exercise can trigger symptoms in some people with asthma.

However, physical activity can still be part of a healthy lifestyle.

People who experience exercise-related asthma symptoms should discuss appropriate prevention and treatment strategies with a health care provider.

# Long-Term Management

Asthma is a chronic condition, so ongoing management may be necessary.

Long-term management can include:

- Taking prescribed controller medicines as directed
- Knowing personal asthma triggers
- Monitoring symptoms
- Using prescribed quick-relief medicines appropriately
- Following an asthma action plan
- Having regular medical follow-up

Treatment may need to be adjusted when symptoms are not adequately controlled.

# When Emergency Care May Be Needed

A severe asthma attack can become life-threatening.

Emergency medical care may be needed when severe breathing difficulty occurs or when prescribed quick-relief treatment does not adequately improve the symptoms.

A person experiencing severe difficulty breathing should seek urgent medical attention rather than relying only on information from this document.

# Important Safety Note

This document provides general medical information for educational purposes.

It does not diagnose asthma, determine whether a person has asthma, or provide a personalized treatment plan.

Asthma medicines, inhaler use, and treatment changes should be discussed with a qualified health care professional.
