# Overview

The common cold is a mild infection of the upper respiratory tract, which includes the nose and throat.

Colds are among the most common illnesses. Adults have an average of two to three colds each year, while children may have more.

Colds can occur at any time of year, although they are often more common during colder seasons.

# Causes

More than 200 different viruses can cause the common cold.

Rhinoviruses are the most common viruses associated with colds.

Because colds are caused by viruses, antibiotics do not treat the common cold.

# How Colds Spread

Cold viruses are highly contagious.

They can spread through:

- Airborne respiratory droplets
- Close personal contact
- Touching contaminated surfaces
- Touching the eyes, nose, or mouth after contact with a virus

A person can become infected after contact with respiratory secretions from someone who has a cold or after touching a contaminated object and then touching their face.

People are often most contagious during the first few days of a cold.

# Symptoms

Common symptoms include:

- Runny nose
- Stuffy or congested nose
- Sneezing
- Sore throat
- Cough
- Headache

Other symptoms can include:

- Fatigue
- Mild fever
- Chills
- Muscle aches
- Decreased appetite
- Postnasal drip

Symptoms often begin a few days after exposure to the virus.

The exact symptoms can vary depending on the virus causing the infection.

# Common Cold and Fever

Adults and older children with a cold often have a low fever or no fever.

Young children may be more likely to develop a fever.

A high or persistent fever may suggest another infection or illness and may require medical evaluation.

# Duration

Most people recover from a common cold within about one to two weeks.

Some symptoms, particularly cough or nasal symptoms, can last longer.

Symptoms that persist without improvement or that become worse after initially improving may require medical evaluation.

# Diagnosis

A common cold is usually diagnosed based on symptoms and medical history.

Most people do not need laboratory testing.

Because symptoms of a cold can overlap with illnesses such as influenza, COVID-19, and allergies, additional evaluation or testing may sometimes be appropriate.

# Treatment

There is no cure that eliminates the common cold.

Treatment focuses on relieving symptoms while the body recovers from the viral infection.

Helpful measures can include:

- Getting adequate rest
- Drinking plenty of fluids
- Using a clean humidifier or cool-mist vaporizer
- Gargling with warm salt water
- Using saline nasal drops or sprays

Over-the-counter medicines may help relieve certain symptoms.

These medicines do not cure the underlying viral infection.

# Over-the-Counter Medicines

Some over-the-counter medicines can help relieve symptoms such as pain, fever, congestion, cough, or sore throat.

People should read medicine labels carefully and follow the instructions.

Some combination cold and cough products contain more than one active ingredient. Taking additional medicines containing the same ingredient can result in taking too much of that medicine.

People taking prescription medicines or managing chronic medical conditions should ask a health care provider or pharmacist which over-the-counter medicines are appropriate.

# Antibiotics

Antibiotics do not treat the common cold.

The common cold is caused by viruses, while antibiotics are medicines used to treat certain bacterial infections.

Taking antibiotics when they are not needed can cause side effects and contributes to antibiotic resistance.

A change in the color of nasal mucus does not by itself mean that antibiotics are needed.

# Children and Cold Medicines

Some cold and cough medicines are not appropriate for young children.

Children and teenagers should not be given aspirin because of the risk of serious complications.

Parents and caregivers should ask a child's health care provider or pharmacist about appropriate medicines and doses.

Medicine labels should always be checked carefully.

# Rest and Fluids

Rest and adequate fluid intake can help support recovery and prevent dehydration.

Warm liquids and salt-water gargles may help relieve some throat symptoms.

A humidifier may help reduce discomfort from dry air, but the device should be kept clean.

# Prevention

There is no vaccine that prevents all common colds because many different viruses can cause them.

Measures that can reduce the risk of getting or spreading a cold include:

- Washing hands frequently with soap and water
- Using alcohol-based hand sanitizer when appropriate
- Avoiding touching the eyes, nose, and mouth with unwashed hands
- Avoiding close contact with people who are sick
- Avoiding sharing cups and eating utensils when someone is sick
- Cleaning frequently touched surfaces
- Covering coughs and sneezes
- Throwing used tissues away
- Staying home when sick

# Common Cold and Asthma

Respiratory infections such as the common cold can trigger wheezing and worsen asthma symptoms in some people with asthma.

People with asthma should follow the asthma management plan provided by their health care professional.

Difficulty breathing during a respiratory infection requires medical attention.

# Possible Complications

Most colds resolve without serious problems.

However, complications can occur.

Possible complications include:

- Bronchitis
- Ear infection
- Sinusitis
- Pneumonia
- Worsening of asthma symptoms

Complications are more likely in certain people, including those with weakened immune systems or underlying respiratory conditions.

# Common Cold vs Flu

The common cold and influenza can have overlapping symptoms.

Cold symptoms commonly include:

- Runny nose
- Nasal congestion
- Sneezing
- Sore throat
- Cough
- Headache

Influenza is caused by influenza viruses and can more commonly cause fever, muscle aches, fatigue, and more sudden illness.

Symptoms alone may not always reliably distinguish a cold from influenza or COVID-19.

Medical testing may be appropriate in some situations.

# When to Seek Medical Care

Medical evaluation may be needed when:

- There is difficulty breathing
- Breathing becomes unusually fast
- Signs of dehydration occur
- Fever lasts longer than four days
- Symptoms last more than ten days without improvement
- Symptoms improve and then return or become worse
- A chronic medical condition becomes worse

Severe symptoms should receive prompt medical evaluation.

# Important Safety Note

This document provides general medical information for educational purposes.

It does not diagnose a common cold or determine whether symptoms are caused by a cold, influenza, COVID-19, allergies, or another condition.

Children, older adults, pregnant people, and people with chronic medical conditions may require different medical guidance.

Medicine selection and dosing should be discussed with an appropriate health care professional when needed.
