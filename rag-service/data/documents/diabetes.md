# Overview

Diabetes, also called diabetes mellitus, is a chronic disease in which blood glucose, or blood sugar, levels become too high.

Glucose is an important source of energy for the body. It comes from the food a person eats and is also produced by the body.

Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas. It helps glucose move from the bloodstream into cells, where it can be used for energy.

In diabetes, the body may not make enough insulin, may not use insulin effectively, or both. As a result, too much glucose remains in the blood.

Persistently high blood glucose can eventually contribute to serious health problems, but appropriate management can help reduce the risk of complications.

# Types of Diabetes

## Type 1 Diabetes

In type 1 diabetes, the body's immune system attacks and destroys the cells in the pancreas that produce insulin.

As a result, the body makes little or no insulin.

Type 1 diabetes can occur at any age but often begins during childhood, adolescence, or young adulthood.

People with type 1 diabetes need insulin every day.

## Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes.

In type 2 diabetes, the body's cells do not respond normally to insulin. This is called insulin resistance.

The pancreas may initially produce more insulin to compensate, but over time it may not be able to produce enough insulin to keep blood glucose within a healthy range.

Type 2 diabetes usually develops gradually, and some people may have few or no noticeable symptoms for a long time.

## Gestational Diabetes

Gestational diabetes is diabetes that develops during pregnancy in a person who did not already have diabetes.

It occurs when the body cannot make enough additional insulin to meet the increased needs of pregnancy.

Gestational diabetes often does not cause noticeable symptoms and is usually detected through screening during pregnancy.

People who have had gestational diabetes have an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes later.

# Causes

Different types of diabetes have different causes.

Type 1 diabetes is associated with an immune system reaction that destroys insulin-producing cells. Genetic and environmental factors may contribute.

Type 2 diabetes results from several factors, including insulin resistance, genetics, and lifestyle-related factors.

Gestational diabetes is associated with hormonal changes during pregnancy together with genetic and lifestyle factors.

# Risk Factors

Risk factors vary depending on the type of diabetes.

Risk factors for type 2 diabetes can include:

- Overweight or obesity
- Physical inactivity
- Family history of diabetes
- Increasing age
- Prediabetes
- Previous gestational diabetes
- Certain medical conditions, including high blood pressure
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Certain patterns of blood lipid levels

Not everyone with type 2 diabetes is overweight or obese.

Risk factors for gestational diabetes can include:

- Overweight or obesity
- Family history of diabetes
- Previous gestational diabetes
- Previous delivery of a large baby
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

# Symptoms

Possible symptoms of diabetes include:

- Increased thirst
- Increased hunger
- Frequent urination, including urination at night
- Fatigue
- Blurred vision
- Numbness or tingling in the hands or feet
- Sores that do not heal
- Unexplained weight loss

Symptoms can vary depending on the type of diabetes.

Symptoms of type 1 diabetes often develop relatively quickly and can become severe.

Type 2 diabetes often develops gradually. Some people may have mild symptoms or no noticeable symptoms for a long period.

Gestational diabetes often causes no symptoms.

# Diabetes and Blood Glucose

Blood glucose, or blood sugar, is the main sugar found in the blood and is an important source of energy.

After food is digested, glucose enters the bloodstream.

Insulin helps glucose move from the bloodstream into cells.

When the body cannot produce enough insulin or cannot use insulin effectively, glucose can build up in the blood.

Persistently high blood glucose can damage blood vessels and organs over time.

# Diagnosis

Health care providers use blood glucose tests to diagnose diabetes.

Common tests include:

## A1C Test

The A1C test measures average blood glucose levels over approximately the previous three months.

An A1C result of 6.5 percent or higher is in the diabetes range.

An A1C result between 5.7 and 6.4 percent is in the prediabetes range.

An A1C result below 5.7 percent is generally considered within the normal range.

## Fasting Blood Glucose Test

A fasting blood glucose test measures blood glucose after a period without eating.

A fasting blood glucose level of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher is in the diabetes range.

## Oral Glucose Tolerance Test

During an oral glucose tolerance test, blood glucose is measured after a person drinks a glucose-containing liquid.

A blood glucose level of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher two hours after the glucose drink is in the diabetes range.

## Random Blood Glucose Test

A random blood glucose test measures blood glucose at a particular time without requiring fasting.

When a person has symptoms of diabetes, a random blood glucose level of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher may indicate diabetes.

A health care provider determines which tests are appropriate and whether testing should be repeated.

# Treatment and Management

Treatment depends on the type of diabetes and the individual's health situation.

## Type 1 Diabetes

People with type 1 diabetes need insulin because their bodies produce little or no insulin.

Insulin can be administered by injection or through an insulin pump.

Blood glucose monitoring is also an important part of diabetes management.

## Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes management may include:

- Healthy eating
- Regular physical activity
- Maintaining or reaching a healthy weight when appropriate
- Monitoring blood glucose
- Diabetes medicines when needed
- Regular medical follow-up

Some people with type 2 diabetes can improve their blood glucose levels substantially through lifestyle changes, while others require medicines.

Medicines can include oral medicines, insulin, and other injectable medicines.

## Gestational Diabetes

Management of gestational diabetes may include healthy eating, physical activity appropriate for pregnancy, blood glucose monitoring, and medicines when needed.

Treatment decisions during pregnancy should be made with a qualified health care provider.

# Blood Glucose Monitoring

Monitoring blood glucose can be an important part of diabetes management.

The appropriate frequency of monitoring depends on the person's type of diabetes, treatment, and medical situation.

Some people use blood glucose meters, while others may use continuous glucose monitoring systems.

A health care provider can recommend an appropriate monitoring approach.

# Low Blood Sugar

Low blood glucose is called hypoglycemia.

For many people with diabetes, a blood glucose reading below 70 mg/dL is considered low, although individual targets may differ.

Possible symptoms include:

- Shaking
- Sweating
- Hunger
- Dizziness
- Nervousness or anxiety
- Irritability
- Confusion

Low blood glucose can become dangerous if it is severe.

People with diabetes should learn from their health care provider how to recognize and manage low blood glucose.

# High Blood Sugar

High blood glucose is called hyperglycemia.

It can occur when the body does not have enough effective insulin or when diabetes is not adequately controlled.

Symptoms of high blood glucose can include increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and blurred vision.

Persistent high blood glucose can contribute to long-term diabetes complications.

# Complications

Over time, diabetes can contribute to several serious health problems.

Possible complications include:

- Eye disease and vision loss
- Kidney disease
- Nerve damage
- Foot problems and infections
- Heart disease
- Heart attack
- Stroke
- Problems with blood circulation
- Increased susceptibility to certain infections

High blood glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves.

Diabetes-related nerve damage may reduce the ability to feel pain, pressure, heat, or cold in the feet. This can make injuries more difficult to notice.

Good management of blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol can help reduce the risk of complications.

# Prevention of Type 2 Diabetes

Type 1 diabetes cannot currently be prevented.

Some cases of type 2 diabetes can be prevented or delayed through lifestyle changes.

Helpful measures can include:

- Regular physical activity
- Healthy eating
- Maintaining a healthy weight
- Losing weight when appropriate for people who are overweight
- Managing other health conditions that increase diabetes risk

People with risk factors for diabetes should discuss screening and prevention with a health care provider.

# Diabetes and Long-Term Monitoring

Diabetes management involves more than monitoring blood glucose.

Regular medical care may include monitoring:

- Blood glucose
- A1C
- Blood pressure
- Cholesterol
- Kidney health
- Eye health
- Foot health

The exact monitoring schedule depends on the individual's type of diabetes and health situation.

# When Medical Attention May Be Needed

A person with diabetes should seek medical advice when symptoms are new, worsening, difficult to control, or when blood glucose repeatedly falls outside the range recommended by their health care provider.

Severe symptoms associated with very low blood glucose can be an emergency.

Severe illness, confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures, or other serious symptoms require urgent medical evaluation.

# Important Safety Note

This document provides general medical information for educational purposes.

It does not diagnose diabetes, determine whether a person has diabetes, or provide a personalized treatment plan.

Blood glucose values and treatment decisions should be interpreted in the context of the individual's health history and by a qualified health care professional.
