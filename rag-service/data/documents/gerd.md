# Overview

Gastroesophageal reflux disease, commonly called GERD, is a chronic condition in which stomach contents move backward from the stomach into the esophagus.

The esophagus is the tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach.

Occasional acid reflux is common. GERD occurs when reflux happens frequently or causes symptoms or damage to the esophagus.

GERD can affect adults, children, and infants.

# How GERD Happens

A ring of muscle at the lower end of the esophagus helps prevent stomach contents from moving backward.

This muscle is called the lower esophageal sphincter (LES).

If the LES becomes weak or relaxes at the wrong time, stomach contents can move back into the esophagus.

Stomach acid and other stomach contents can irritate the lining of the esophagus and cause symptoms.

# Causes and Risk Factors

GERD can occur when the lower esophageal sphincter does not close properly.

Factors that can increase the likelihood of GERD include:

- Overweight or obesity
- Pregnancy
- Hiatal hernia
- Smoking
- Exposure to secondhand smoke
- Lying down soon after eating

Certain foods, drinks, and medicines can also make reflux symptoms worse.

# Common Symptoms

The most common symptom of GERD is heartburn.

Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest or throat that occurs when stomach contents move upward into the esophagus.

Other possible symptoms include:

- Acid or food coming back into the mouth
- Regurgitation
- Dry cough
- Hoarse voice
- Asthma-like symptoms
- Trouble swallowing
- Sore throat
- Nausea
- Hiccups

A person can have GERD without experiencing heartburn.

Symptoms may become worse after eating, when bending over, or when lying down.

Symptoms can also be worse at night.

# Heartburn and GERD

Heartburn is a common symptom of acid reflux.

Occasional heartburn does not necessarily mean that a person has GERD.

Frequent heartburn, especially when it occurs repeatedly or causes other problems, may be associated with GERD.

GERD can also occur without typical heartburn.

# Diagnosis

A health care provider may diagnose GERD based on symptoms and medical history.

Some people with mild symptoms may not need diagnostic tests.

When additional evaluation is needed, tests can include:

- Upper GI endoscopy
- Esophageal pH testing
- Esophageal manometry
- Esophageal impedance testing
- Upper gastrointestinal imaging in selected situations

# Upper GI Endoscopy

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy allows a health care provider to examine the lining of the esophagus and stomach using a flexible tube with a small camera.

A biopsy may be performed during an endoscopy when tissue needs to be examined.

Endoscopy may be recommended when symptoms are severe, persistent, or return after treatment, or when complications are suspected.

# Esophageal pH Testing

Esophageal pH testing measures how often stomach acid enters the esophagus and how long acid remains there.

This testing can help evaluate whether symptoms are associated with acid reflux.

# Esophageal Manometry

Esophageal manometry measures pressure and movement in the esophagus.

It can help evaluate how well the esophagus and lower esophageal sphincter function.

# Treatment

Most people can manage GERD with lifestyle changes and medicines.

Treatment depends on the person's symptoms and medical situation.

Treatment options may include:

- Lifestyle changes
- Over-the-counter medicines
- Prescription medicines
- In some cases, procedures or surgery

# Lifestyle Management

Lifestyle measures that may help reduce GERD symptoms include:

- Eating smaller meals
- Avoiding lying down for at least 2 to 3 hours after eating
- Avoiding foods that trigger symptoms
- Losing weight when appropriate
- Raising the head of the bed when symptoms occur at night
- Quitting smoking
- Avoiding alcohol when it worsens symptoms

Common foods or drinks that may trigger symptoms in some people include:

- Spicy foods
- Fatty foods
- Chocolate
- Caffeine

Triggers vary between individuals.

# Medicines

Several types of medicines can be used to manage GERD.

## Antacids

Antacids neutralize stomach acid and can provide short-term relief from heartburn.

They do not treat all causes or complications of GERD.

## H2 Blockers

H2 blockers reduce the amount of acid produced by the stomach.

They may provide longer-lasting relief than antacids.

## Proton Pump Inhibitors

Proton pump inhibitors, or PPIs, reduce stomach acid production.

They are commonly used to treat GERD and acid-related damage to the esophagus.

Medication choice and duration should be determined with appropriate medical guidance.

## Potassium-Competitive Acid Blockers

Potassium-competitive acid blockers, or P-CABs, are another class of medicines that reduce stomach acid.

A health care provider can determine whether a particular medicine is appropriate.

# GERD and Medications

Some medicines can worsen heartburn or reflux symptoms.

Examples can include certain medicines used for:

- High blood pressure
- Heart disease
- Asthma
- Parkinson disease
- Anxiety or insomnia

A person should not stop or change a prescribed medicine without discussing it with a health care professional.

# Complications

Untreated or persistent GERD can sometimes cause complications.

Possible complications include:

- Inflammation of the esophagus
- Esophageal ulcers
- Scarring and narrowing of the esophagus
- Barrett's esophagus
- Chronic cough
- Chronic hoarseness
- Worsening of asthma symptoms
- Dental problems

Barrett's esophagus involves changes in the lining of the esophagus and can increase the risk of esophageal cancer.

# GERD and Asthma

GERD can be associated with asthma-like symptoms and may worsen asthma in some people.

Persistent cough or breathing-related symptoms should not automatically be assumed to be caused by GERD because other conditions can cause similar symptoms.

# Prevention and Symptom Reduction

Measures that may help reduce GERD symptoms include:

- Maintaining a healthy body weight
- Eating smaller meals
- Avoiding personal trigger foods
- Avoiding meals close to bedtime
- Remaining upright after eating
- Avoiding tobacco
- Limiting alcohol when it worsens symptoms
- Raising the head of the bed when nighttime symptoms occur

Not every measure works equally well for every person.

# When to Contact a Health Care Provider

Medical evaluation may be appropriate when:

- GERD symptoms occur frequently
- Symptoms do not improve with lifestyle changes
- Symptoms continue despite treatment
- Food feels stuck when swallowing
- Swallowing is painful or difficult
- There is unexplained weight loss
- There is frequent vomiting
- There is loss of appetite
- There is bleeding
- Persistent cough or hoarseness occurs

# Emergency Warning Signs

Chest pain can have causes other than GERD.

Chest pain accompanied by shortness of breath or pain in the jaw or arm can be a sign of a heart problem and requires urgent medical attention.

Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools can also require urgent medical evaluation.

# Important Safety Note

This document provides general medical information for educational purposes.

It does not diagnose GERD or determine the cause of a person's symptoms.

Chest pain should not automatically be assumed to be heartburn or GERD.

Medication changes and treatment decisions should be made with a qualified health care professional.
