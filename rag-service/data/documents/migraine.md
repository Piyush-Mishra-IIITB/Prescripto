# Overview

A migraine is a recurring type of headache that can cause moderate to severe pain. The pain is often throbbing or pulsing and is frequently felt on one side of the head.

Migraine is a neurological condition that can cause symptoms in addition to headache. These may include nausea, vomiting, sensitivity to light and sound, dizziness, weakness, and changes in vision.

Some people experience migraine attacks with an aura, while others have migraine without aura.

# Causes

The exact sequence of events that causes a migraine is not completely understood.

Genetic factors appear to play an important role. Many people with migraine have family members who also experience migraines.

Migraine attacks can be triggered by different factors, and triggers vary from person to person.

# Common Migraine Triggers

Possible triggers include:

- Stress
- Anxiety
- Hormonal changes
- Changes in sleep patterns
- Too little or too much sleep
- Skipping meals
- Dehydration
- Bright or flashing lights
- Loud noises
- Strong smells
- Sudden weather or environmental changes
- Physical overexertion
- Tobacco smoke
- Caffeine
- Caffeine withdrawal
- Alcohol
- Certain medicines
- Medication overuse

Some people also report certain foods as triggers, including chocolate, aged cheeses, processed meats, fermented or pickled foods, and foods containing MSG.

Triggers are highly individual. A food or activity that triggers a migraine in one person may not trigger one in another person.

# Risk Factors

Migraine can affect people of all ages.

Factors associated with a higher likelihood of migraine include:

- Family history of migraine
- Being female
- Hormonal changes
- Certain sleep disorders
- Depression or anxiety
- Bipolar disorder
- Epilepsy

Migraine can occur in children as well as adults.

# Types of Migraine

Two common forms are:

- Migraine with aura
- Migraine without aura

Migraine without aura is common and occurs without the neurological warning symptoms known as aura.

# Migraine Phases

A migraine attack can occur in several phases.

A person does not necessarily experience every phase during every migraine attack.

## Prodrome

The prodrome phase can occur before the headache.

Possible symptoms include:

- Food cravings
- Mood changes
- Yawning
- Fatigue
- Increased urination
- Fluid retention

Prodrome symptoms can begin hours before the headache.

## Aura

Aura consists of temporary neurological symptoms that can occur before or during a migraine.

Visual symptoms can include:

- Flashing lights
- Zigzag lines
- Bright spots
- Blind spots
- Changes in vision

Other aura symptoms can include:

- Numbness
- Tingling
- Weakness
- Difficulty finding words
- Changes in sensation

Not everyone with migraine experiences aura.

## Headache Phase

The headache phase often begins gradually and can become more severe.

The pain is commonly:

- Throbbing
- Pulsing
- Moderate to severe
- More noticeable on one side of the head

Other symptoms may include:

- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Sensitivity to light
- Sensitivity to sound
- Sensitivity to odors
- Pain that becomes worse with movement

Some people can experience migraine symptoms without having a typical headache.

## Postdrome

After the headache ends, some people experience a postdrome phase.

Possible symptoms include:

- Fatigue
- Weakness
- Difficulty concentrating
- Feeling mentally unclear
- Neck discomfort

# Duration

Migraine headaches can last from several hours to several days.

A typical untreated migraine attack can last approximately 4 to 72 hours.

The exact duration varies between individuals and attacks.

# Diagnosis

There is no single laboratory test that proves a person has migraine.

A health care provider may diagnose migraine by evaluating:

- Symptoms
- Headache pattern
- Medical history
- Family history
- Physical examination
- Neurological examination

The provider may also evaluate other possible causes of headache.

# Diagnostic Tests

Most people with typical migraine symptoms do not require special imaging tests.

Additional tests may be considered when symptoms are unusual or when another medical condition needs to be ruled out.

Possible tests include:

- CT scan
- MRI
- Blood tests
- Electroencephalogram (EEG) in selected situations
- Lumbar puncture in selected situations

The need for testing depends on the person's symptoms and medical history.

# Treatment

There is currently no cure for migraine, but treatment can help relieve symptoms and reduce the frequency or severity of attacks.

Treatment can include:

- Medicines to treat an acute migraine attack
- Medicines to prevent migraine attacks
- Lifestyle changes
- Trigger management
- Rest and hydration

Treatment should be selected with consideration of the person's medical history and other medicines.

# Treating a Migraine Attack

During a migraine attack, some people may benefit from:

- Resting in a quiet, dark room
- Closing the eyes
- Drinking fluids
- Using a cool cloth or ice pack on the forehead

For some people, medicines may also be used.

Over-the-counter pain medicines can sometimes help with mild migraine symptoms.

Prescription medicines may be used when over-the-counter medicines are not effective or when migraines are more severe.

Some prescription migraine medicines include triptans and medicines that target calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP).

A health care provider can determine which treatment is appropriate.

# Preventive Treatment

Preventive treatment may be considered when migraine attacks are frequent or significantly interfere with daily life.

Preventive medicines may include certain:

- Blood pressure medicines
- Antidepressants
- Anti-seizure medicines
- CGRP-targeting medicines

Botulinum toxin type A may be used for selected people with chronic migraine.

Treatment decisions should be made with a health care professional.

# Lifestyle Management

Lifestyle measures can help some people reduce migraine frequency or severity.

Helpful strategies may include:

- Maintaining a regular sleep schedule
- Eating regular meals
- Staying adequately hydrated
- Managing stress
- Exercising regularly when appropriate
- Avoiding known personal triggers
- Avoiding tobacco
- Limiting or avoiding alcohol when it triggers migraines
- Managing caffeine intake

A headache diary can help identify patterns and possible triggers.

# Headache Diary

A headache diary can record:

- Date and time the headache started
- What was eaten and drank
- Amount and quality of sleep
- Activities before the headache
- Possible triggers
- Symptoms experienced
- Medicines taken
- How long the headache lasted
- What helped relieve the symptoms

Sharing this information with a health care provider can help with diagnosis and treatment planning.

# Medication Overuse

Using headache medicines too frequently can cause medication-overuse headaches, sometimes called rebound headaches.

Repeated use of pain medicines can cause headaches to become more frequent or difficult to control.

People who regularly need headache medicine several days per week should discuss this with a health care provider.

# Migraine and Hormones

Hormonal changes can trigger migraine attacks in some people.

Some people experience migraines around menstruation.

Hormonal changes during pregnancy or with hormonal medicines can also affect migraine patterns.

Hormonal factors vary significantly between individuals.

# When to Seek Medical Attention

A headache should receive urgent medical evaluation when it:

- Starts suddenly and is extremely severe
- Is described as the worst headache of a person's life
- Occurs with new problems speaking
- Occurs with new vision problems
- Occurs with weakness or loss of movement
- Occurs with loss of balance
- Occurs with confusion or loss of alertness
- Occurs with fever
- Is substantially different from previous headaches

These symptoms can sometimes indicate a condition other than migraine.

# Important Safety Note

This document provides general medical information for educational purposes.

It does not diagnose migraine or determine the cause of a person's headache.

A new, sudden, unusually severe, or significantly different headache should not automatically be assumed to be a migraine.

Medication selection, dosage, and preventive treatment should be discussed with a qualified health care professional.
